01 / The Challenge

Good decisions need clear information.

Before choosing an event, visitors may need to understand the full ticket price and whether relevant accessibility information is available. When these details are difficult to compare, making a decision can involve unnecessary uncertainty.

Product opportunity

Design a simple event-discovery experience that prioritizes upfront pricing and accessibility information while preserving an engaging visual experience.

02 / Product Strategy

People first. Clarity by design.

The concept focuses on visitors who want to compare events without navigating a complicated checkout process.

GOAL 01

Show the full price

Include sample mandatory fees in the advertised ticket price and display an understandable breakdown.

GOAL 02

Improve information access

Surface available step-free access information during event discovery, rather than hiding it in later steps.

GOAL 03

Simplify decisions

Create a focused journey that moves from discovery to price comparison and simulated confirmation.

Research and scope

The concept is informed by the broader consumer issue of ticket-fee transparency. Its proposed accessibility-information needs are design hypotheses. No user interviews or external usability studies were conducted for this project.

05 / Visual Design

01 / Typography and hierarchy

Large, bold headlines establish a recognizable visual identity, while smaller labels organize event information. The interface prioritizes clarity over unnecessary decorative complexity.

02 / Color and identity

Forest green anchors navigation and primary actions. Lime reinforces the brand illustration, while orange and lavender differentiate event categories.

03 / Original graphic elements

Event artwork uses geometric shapes built with CSS instead of copied concert posters, logos, or promotional photography. This keeps the visual language consistent and independently created.

04 / Action hierarchy

Primary buttons identify the next step, while secondary actions allow visitors to return and reconsider their selection.

05 / Motion and interaction feedback

I introduced subtle motion to make Event Clarity feel more engaging while keeping the experience easy to navigate. The hero ticket uses a gentle floating animation, while event cards and buttons provide visual feedback on hover. These effects reinforce the visual identity and communicate interactivity without distracting from important event information. Decorative motion is disabled for visitors who prefer reduced motion.

06 / Interactive Prototype

From concept to interaction.

I implemented the consumer experience using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, connecting the three stages into one functional demonstration.

Event discovery

Fictional event cards with categories, original graphics, and upfront total prices.

Interactive filters

Visitors can filter by event category and whether step-free access is listed.

Dynamic pricing

Ticket quantity changes the displayed subtotal, mandatory demo fees, and total.

Simulated confirmation

A clear final state summarizes the selection without collecting payment or personal data.

07 / Functional Evaluation

Tested for expected behavior.

Version 1 passed eight self-conducted functional checks covering the three-stage experience and responsive behavior.

DISCOVERY

Filters worked

Event cards displayed correctly, including all-in prices, and category and access-information filters behaved as expected.

INTERACTION

Pricing updated

The selected event opened correctly, and changing ticket quantity updated the displayed total.

COMPLETION

The journey worked

Simulated confirmation, returning to discovery, and the tested mobile layout all worked as expected.

Evaluation limitations

These checks verify expected prototype behavior. They are not external usability testing, and no participant performance measures or business impact results are claimed. Comprehensive WCAG conformance has not been established.

08 / Reflection

Simple interactions. Purposeful decisions.

Event Clarity demonstrates how a small consumer product can bring together visual identity, interface hierarchy, accessible information, and functioning interactions within a focused scope.

The next design opportunity would be testing the experience with prospective eventgoers, gathering feedback on pricing comprehension, and refining the interface using those findings.
